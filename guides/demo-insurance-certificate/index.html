What insurance a brand needs for an in-store demo

What a certificate of insurance is, what stores usually ask for, and what to check before a demo is confirmed.

Before a brand samples product in a grocery store, the store will almost always ask for proof of insurance. This guide explains what that proof is, what stores commonly ask for, and what a store should check before confirming a demo. It is general information, not legal or insurance advice. Your broker and the store's own requirements have the final word.

What a certificate of insurance is

A certificate of insurance, often shortened to COI, is a one page summary issued by an insurance broker or carrier. It shows who is insured, what kinds of coverage they carry, the limits, and the dates the policy is in force. It is usually on a standard form called an ACORD 25. A brand asks its broker for one, and most brokers send it the same day at no charge.

The coverage stores usually ask for

For a sampling demo, the coverage that matters is commercial general liability. A common requirement at independent grocers is a limit of $1 million per occurrence and $2 million in aggregate. Some larger chains ask for more. Small and new brands can usually meet the $1 million and $2 million level, which is one reason many independent stores settle on it.

Umbrella coverage is usually optional. Liquor liability coverage is a separate matter and only comes up when the store has approved a demo that involves alcohol.

Certificate holder and additional insured

These two terms cause most of the confusion.

  • Certificate holder is the party the certificate is addressed to. Naming the store as certificate holder shows the store that the coverage exists. It does not give the store any rights under the policy.
  • Additional insured means the store is added to the brand's policy for claims arising from the brand's activity. It is usually done by an endorsement, and it gives the store real protection.

Stores differ on which they ask for. If a store asks to be named as additional insured, the brand's broker can usually add it quickly. Ask for the exact name and address the store wants on the certificate.

What a store should check

  • The insured name. It should match the company running the demo. That is either the brand itself or the demo agency the brand has hired.
  • The dates. The policy has to be in force on the day of the demo, not only on the day the certificate was sent.
  • The limits. They should meet the amounts your store asks for.
  • How your store is named, if you ask to be listed as certificate holder or additional insured.

When a demo agency runs the demo

Many brands hire a demo agency to staff their tables. In that case the agency's own certificate often covers the person at the table, and a store may accept it. Some stores want the brand's certificate as well, since the brand makes the product. Decide which your store requires and say so in your demo guidelines.

Demos that need more than the standard certificate

Four kinds of demos carry more risk, and most stores allow them only with specific approval: alcohol, open flame or cooking, raw or unpasteurized products, and CBD or hemp products. Alcohol in particular usually needs liquor liability coverage and has to follow state rules on tastings.

Keep track of the expiry date

Policies renew once a year, so a certificate that was fine in spring can be expired by fall. Record the expiry date when you receive the certificate, and ask for a new one before it lapses. Checking this when a demo is booked is far easier than discovering it on the day.

The short version: ask every brand for a certificate showing general liability coverage, commonly $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate. Check the name, the dates and the limits, and keep the expiry date on file.
