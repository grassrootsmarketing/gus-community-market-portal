How to run a brand demo program at an independent grocery store

The nine decisions that turn a pile of emails and texts into a demo program your whole team can follow.

Brand demos are good for a store. Shoppers try something new, a local brand gets a fair shot, and the product moves. The hard part is rarely the demo. It is the scheduling around it: the email threads, the rep who shows up unannounced, the certificate nobody can find, and the staff who did not know a table was coming.

A demo program does not need to be complicated. It needs a few decisions made once and written down. This guide walks through them in the order most stores hit them.

1. Decide when and where you host demos

Pick the days and time slots that work for each location, and stick to them. Most stores choose weekend slots and a late afternoon slot on weekdays, when traffic is high and staff can spare the floor space. Decide how many demos you can host at once. One table per department is a common limit.

If you have more than one location, set the hours for each store separately. A slot that works at your largest store may not work at your smallest.

2. Set a lead time

Ask brands to book a set number of days ahead. Two weeks is a sensible default. It gives your buyer time to make sure the product is ordered and on the shelf, and it stops last minute requests from landing on a busy store manager.

3. Decide whether to charge a demo fee

Charging a demo fee is optional, and plenty of independent stores charge nothing. Larger chains often charge brands to schedule through their vendor portals. If you do charge, keep it to one flat amount per demo and collect it when the brand books, so nobody at your store is chasing an invoice afterward.

4. Ask for insurance before the demo, not on the day

Most stores ask every brand for a certificate of insurance showing general liability coverage. The moment to collect it is when the brand books, so a missing or expired certificate is found two weeks out and not at the front door. Keep the certificate on file with its expiry date, so the next booking from the same brand does not start from zero. Our guide to demo insurance covers what to look for.

5. Put your demo guidelines in writing

Write down what you expect: when to arrive, where to set up, dress and food handling expectations, what the brand brings, and how the space should be left. Say which kinds of demos need your specific approval first. Alcohol, open flame or cooking, raw or unpasteurized products, and CBD are the usual four. Have each brand agree to the guidelines when it books, and keep a record of that agreement.

6. Know exactly what is being sampled

Ask the brand to list the items it will sample, with the item name, UPC and distributor. This is the detail that makes a demo pay off. A demo for a product that is out of stock, or was never ordered, sells nothing. With the list in hand a week ahead, your buyer can check the shelf and place an order in time.

7. Tell the people who work that day

The store manager who approved the demo is often not the person on the floor when the rep arrives. Send the details to the people at that location: which brand, which items, what date and time. A reminder a week before, another three days before, and one on the morning of the demo covers almost every surprise.

8. Keep every demo on one calendar

One calendar, for all locations, that anyone on your team can look at. It prevents double bookings, shows open slots at a glance, and gives you a record of which brands demo often and which have gone quiet.

9. Have a simple cancellation rule

Decide in advance what happens when a brand cancels. A common rule is a full refund of any demo fee up to a few days before the demo, and no refund after that. Write it into your guidelines so the answer is the same every time.

The short version: set your hours and lead time once, collect insurance and a product list at booking, send your staff the details, and keep one calendar. Everything else is detail.
